The Real History of the Landlord’s Game

Monopoly wasn’t built to be played the way we play it now. Around 1904, a Washington, D.C. stenographer named Elizabeth “Lizzie” Magie designed a board game called The Landlord’s Game and patented it on January 5, 1904. Magie followed the idea that land, unlike labor or capital, shouldn’t be privately profited from, since nobody actually creates land. Her game was meant to make that argument playable.

The original board actually had two rulesets. Under the “Prosperity” rules, rent collected from developed land went into a shared pot, and everyone’s fortunes rose together. Under the “Monopolist” rules (the version we still play), the goal was to bankrupt everyone else and own it all. Magie wanted people to play both back to back and feel which system actually worked better.

It never took off under her name. She self-published it and pitched it to Parker Brothers in 1909, who turned it down for being too political and too complicated. So it spread the slow way with hand-drawn copies passed through Quaker communities in Atlantic City and college economics classes across the Northeast. A Wharton professor named Scott Nearing even used it to teach students about the flaws of industrial capitalism, until he was fired in 1915 for exactly that kind of thing.

By the early 1930s, an unemployed Philadelphia salesman named Charles Darrow learned a hand-copied version from friends, redrew it with Atlantic City street names, and started selling it as his own. He patented it, sold it to Parker Brothers in 1935, and got credited as Monopoly’s sole inventor. Parker Brothers knew about Magie’s original patent but they just bought it from her quietly for about $500 flat, no royalties, and let her disappear from the story for decades.

Her name didn’t resurface publicly until 1973, when Parker Brothers sued economics professor Ralph Anspach over his own game, Anti-Monopoly. Building his defense, Anspach dug up Magie’s 1904 patent, and her role finally made it into the record.
