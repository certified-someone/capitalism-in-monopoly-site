Bankruptcy · Winners, Losers, and Inequality

Bankruptcy

Every game of Monopoly ends with one winner and everyone else bankrupt. That’s not an accident.

Go To Jail

Every Monopoly game ends the same way with one player owning everything, everyone else being bankrupt, fully out, not just behind. That’s not a glitch, it’s the point of the rules. The real economy has followed a similar path since the 1970s: the income gap between world regions now sits around 19 to 1, up from as low as 13 to 1. Between individual countries, it’s gone from about 3 to 1 in the 1800s to somewhere around 72 to 1 today.

Sociologist Manuel Castells tracked a similar shift in global income shares: between the 1960s and 1990s, the poorest 20% of the world went from holding 2.3% of global income to just 1.4%, while the richest 20% climbed from 70% to 85%. Consumption tells the same story as the wealthiest fifth accounts for about 86% of all private spending, the poorest fifth just 1.3%. That same top fifth eats 45% of the world’s meat and fish and burns 58% of its energy; the poorest fifth gets 5% and 4%.

The Rules of the Game

“Growth doesn’t lift every player. It was never built to.”

None of this is an accident, and it’s not a bug in an otherwise fair system. It follows straight from everything on the earlier pages: a system built to manufacture demand, backed by a state that makes it feel natural, scaled by institutions that answer to a narrow elite.

Where do you land in this system of producers, consumers, or both? If bankruptcy in Monopoly is a design choice and not an accident, what would it take to design the real version differently?
